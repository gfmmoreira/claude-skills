# Reality Check Framework

Use these selectively. Don't run through them as a checklist — apply what's relevant to the specific topic. The goal is to separate what's real from what's constructed, so the user shows up with accurate framing rather than a defended position.

---

## Observation vs. Interpretation

When the user makes a claim about someone's motives, attitude, or character:

- "What specifically happened that led you to that conclusion?"
- "If I asked them to describe the same situation, what would they say?"
- "Is that what happened, or is that what it meant to you?"

Red flags: words like "always," "never," "clearly," "obviously," "they just don't..." — these usually signal interpretation dressed as fact.

---

## Evidence Gaps

When a claim can't be grounded in something observable:

- "How many times has this actually happened?"
- "What would change your mind about this?"
- "Is this a pattern or is this the one time it happened?"

If the user can't answer these, the claim isn't ready to bring to the meeting. Note it as ungrounded and either drop it or reframe it as a question.

---

## The Other Person's Perspective

Always offer this, even unprompted:

- "What pressures or constraints might they be operating under that you're not fully seeing?"
- "If they're doing this intentionally, what would their reason be? If unintentionally, what explains it?"
- "Is it possible they don't know this is a problem for you?"

This isn't about defending them. It's about preventing the user from walking in with a story that will collapse the moment the other person opens their mouth.

---

## User's Contribution

The hardest one. Apply it when the complaint has a two-sided dynamic:

- "What part of this situation are you contributing to?"
- "Is there something you could have done differently that might have changed this outcome?"
- "Have you actually said this to them before, clearly?"

Deliver this directly but without judgment. The goal isn't to make the user feel bad — it's to prevent them from raising an issue as if it's entirely the other person's problem when it isn't.

---

## Emotional Readiness

If the topic is charged:

- "Can you raise this without it sounding like an accusation?"
- "If they get defensive, what happens to you?"
- "Are you looking for a resolution or do you need to vent first?"

If the user is not emotionally regulated on a topic, they shouldn't raise it until they are. A conversation held from anger or hurt rarely lands the way it's intended.

---

## Timing and Setting

- "Is a 1-on-1 the right place for this, or should it be in writing / a separate conversation / not raised yet?"
- "What needs to be true before this conversation can go well?"
- "Is there a better moment — after a specific event, review cycle, milestone?"

---

## Pattern Recognition (if previous notes available)

- Has this topic come up before without resolution?
- If the same issue keeps surfacing, the question isn't how to say it better — it's why it hasn't been resolved yet.
- Flag recurring themes explicitly: "This has come up in at least [X] previous meetings. What's the actual blocker to resolving it?"
